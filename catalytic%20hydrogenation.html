Catalytic hydrogenation

How hydrogen and a metal catalyst reduce carbon–carbon multiple bonds—and why an aromatic benzene ring behaves differently from a cyclohexadiene.

1. The core idea

Catalytic hydrogenation adds hydrogen across unsaturated bonds. In a typical reaction, H₂ contacts a metal surface such as palladium, platinum, or nickel, where the H–H bond is activated and hydrogen atoms can add to the substrate.

C=C + H₂ → C–C   (metal catalyst)

Reagent

Hydrogen gas, H₂. One mole of H₂ is consumed per ordinary C=C bond reduced.

Catalyst

Common examples: Pd/C, Pt, or Raney Ni. The catalyst provides a surface pathway; it is not normally consumed stoichiometrically.

Outcome

The π bond is removed and two C–H σ bonds form. The carbon framework usually remains intact.

Key distinction: a normal alkene is often hydrogenated readily. Benzene is aromatic and unusually stabilized, so hydrogenating its ring generally requires more forcing conditions than hydrogenating an isolated alkene.

3. A simplified surface mechanism

For a common heterogeneous catalyst, the reaction occurs at the metal surface. The exact details vary with metal, solvent, and substrate; this is a useful introductory model.

  1. Adsorption: H₂ and the unsaturated molecule approach and bind to the catalyst surface.
  2. Hydrogen activation: H₂ dissociates on the metal surface into adsorbed hydrogen atoms (often written H*).
  3. π-bond interaction: the alkene or diene associates with the surface, making its π bond available for reaction.
  4. Hydrogen transfer: hydrogen atoms add to the two carbon atoms of a C=C bond. The π bond is converted to a C–C σ bond.
  5. Desorption: the saturated product leaves the surface, freeing catalyst sites for another cycle.
H₂ + 2 * ⇌ 2 H*
alkene + 2 H* → alkane + 2 *

* denotes a vacant catalyst surface site; H* is hydrogen adsorbed on that surface.

Stereochemical rule: because hydrogen atoms are delivered from a surface-bound arrangement, addition to a simple alkene is commonly described as syn addition. In rings, this can matter for the relative stereochemistry of substituents and newly added hydrogens.

Aromaticity

Why benzene is special

Benzene is stabilized by a delocalized six-π-electron system. Hydrogenation converts its sp² ring carbons toward sp³ carbons and removes that aromatic system, so the reaction has a larger energetic barrier than reducing a typical isolated alkene.

Conjugated diene

Why partial reduction may occur

A diene has two π bonds. If conditions or catalyst selectivity allow, one can be reduced before the other. With ordinary excess hydrogen and an active catalyst, further reduction to the alkane is often possible.

5. Practical rules and selectivity

  • Count the bonds: each ordinary C=C reduced consumes one mole of H₂; each C≡C reduced completely to C–C consumes two moles of H₂.
  • Do not equate “one ring” with “one equivalent”: benzene requires three H₂ equivalents to become cyclohexane because it has three formal π bonds in a Kekulé drawing.
  • Aromatic rings are different: benzene's aromatic stabilization makes ring hydrogenation more demanding than ordinary alkene reduction.
  • Catalysts are not interchangeable: Pd/C, Pt, and nickel can differ in activity and chemoselectivity. Catalyst poisoning and additives can alter behavior.
  • Other functional groups may react: depending on conditions, nitro groups, alkynes, carbonyls, benzyl protecting groups, and other groups can be affected. Consider the whole molecule.
  • Substituents matter: steric hindrance, adsorption geometry, solvent, pressure, temperature, and catalyst surface can affect rate and product distribution.
Safety: hydrogen is highly flammable, and hydrogenation catalysts can be hazardous; some supported catalysts may ignite when dry or exposed to air after use. Laboratory hydrogenation requires appropriate equipment, ventilation, pressure controls, and approved procedures.

6. Worked examples

Example A — benzene to cyclohexane

C₆H₆ + 3 H₂ → C₆H₁₂

Three H₂ molecules are added overall. Each of the six ring carbons changes from approximately sp² to sp³ hybridization, and the aromatic π system is removed.

Example B — 1,3-cyclohexadiene to cyclohexane

C₆H₈ + 2 H₂ → C₆H₁₂

Two C=C bonds are reduced, so two H₂ equivalents are needed for complete saturation. With one equivalent under suitable selective conditions, a cyclohexene product may form, though product mixtures are possible.

Example C — a substituted ring

For a methyl-substituted cyclohexadiene, the ring's two C=C bonds can be reduced while the methyl group remains unchanged under typical alkene-hydrogenation conditions. If the substituent contains another reducible group, its fate must be evaluated separately.

7. Quick check

Question: How many equivalents of H₂ are required to convert one molecule of benzene fully to cyclohexane?

Answer: 3 equivalents of H₂. Benzene has three formal π bonds in a Kekulé representation, and full hydrogenation produces cyclohexane (C₆H₁₂) from benzene (C₆H₆).

Try another: A cyclohexadiene has two C=C bonds. How many H₂ equivalents are needed to reduce both?

Answer: 2 equivalents of H₂ for complete reduction of the two C=C bonds to a saturated ring.
